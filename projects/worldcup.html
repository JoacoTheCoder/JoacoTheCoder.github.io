308 goals, and the columns that made them interesting

Open the live dashboard →  ·  Code on GitHub

What it does now

Four views behind a sidebar. Overview covers when goals were scored and how the 308 accumulated. Goal Insights covers who scores — by age, by position, by name. Country takes one team and plots every goal it scored by match and minute, coloured by whether that team was losing, drawing or winning at that moment. Venue takes one stadium and places it among the sixteen. A dual-handle minute slider scopes every chart to a window of the match, and because it runs on the derived sort key, a window ending at 90 still contains the 90+4 goals.

Why I built it

We all know how it ended. Spain on top, and I watched the whole thing the way a fan watches — pure passion, no notebook.

When it was over I wanted to keep living in it, just through a different lens. So I went to the data to ask whether it told the same story I'd just watched. Some of it did. Some of it didn't.

The source is thin on purpose

The feed is public domain and complete, which is the good news. It is also ten fields per match, with goals as bare {name, minute} objects: 

{
  "round": "Matchday 1",
  "date": "2026-06-11",
  "time": "13:00 UTC-6",
  "team1": "Mexico", "team2": "South Africa",
  "score": { "ft": [2, 0], "ht": [1, 0] },
  "goals1": [{ "name": "Julián Quiñones", "minute": "9" }],
  "goals2": [],
  "group": "Group A",
  "ground": "Mexico City"
}

One match, as the feed gives it to you.

Chart that directly and you get what everyone gets: goals per team, goals per minute. The questions I actually wanted — do trailing teams push late, does the first goal decide the match, did the roofed stadiums play differently — need columns that don't exist yet.

Score state: replaying the match

The one that carries the project. Every goal is re-run in chronological order and stamped with the score that existed the moment before it was struck: 

running = [0, 0]
for event in sorted(events, key=lambda e: e["order"]):
    before_for     = running[event["side"] - 1]
    before_against = running[2 - event["side"]]
    event["score_state"] = ("Leading"  if before_for >  before_against else
                            "Level"    if before_for == before_against else
                            "Trailing")
    running[event["side"] - 1] += 1

It's a windowed state calculation over an ordered event stream — the same shape as a running total in SQL. It turns a flat list of goals into something you can interrogate.

The finding inverts the obvious guess. From the 85th minute on, 45% of goals were scored by a team already ahead and only 22% by a team behind — against 34% leading across the match as a whole. Teams chasing a game push players forward, and the team in front punishes the space they leave.

I expected the opposite. That's the point of deriving the column instead of assuming the answer.

Four traps I had to handle

1. ft is not the final score

It's the score after 90 minutes. Five matches went to extra time and four of those to penalties, so anything keyed on ft alone calls the wrong winner — including both quarter-finals that went long. I resolve the result as shootout, then extra time, then regulation, so Belgium–Senegal reads ft [2,2] in the feed and 3–2 on the dashboard. Extra-time goals keep their real minute, which is why the slider runs to 120.

2. Own goals are credited to the beneficiary

Miro Muheim is Swiss but appears under goals1 of Qatar v Switzerland. So the team credit is already right — but the player played for the other side. Attribute the goal to his listed team and you misassign 14 goals in every player-level cut. I carry team and scorer_team as separate columns, and I drop own goals from every position and scorer cut: a defender putting through his own net is not evidence about how often defenders score.

3. Stoppage time is not the minute it follows

"90+4" has to sort after the 90th minute and before the 91st, and must never collapse into 90 — a stoppage-time winner is a different event from a 90th-minute one. I parse it as (base, stoppage, base + stoppage/1000), so it sorts correctly and a window ending at 90 still contains it.

4. The stadium file mixes three coordinate notations

49°16'36"N, 40°48'48.7"N and 37.403°N all appear in the same file. I wrote the parser to take all three, because handling only the first drops four venues off the map without erroring — the failure mode I'd never have caught by looking at the chart.

Validation, because a silent break is the real risk

The pipeline refuses to write anything until twenty-three assertions pass. Several check against facts published elsewhere, so an upstream change can't slip through unnoticed: 

[ok] matches                          104
[ok] goals                            308
[ok] golden boot scorer               Kylian Mbappé
[ok] France goals                     20
[ok] champion                         Spain
[ok] goals reconcile to match totals  308

Plus a per-match reconciliation: the flattened goal list has to reproduce every scoreline exactly or the build fails naming the match. That check is what confirmed the own-goal behaviour above — if my reading had been wrong, 14 matches would not have reconciled.

Choices I'd defend

  • Standard library only. No pandas, no venv, no wheels to fail on a new interpreter. It runs on any Python 3 on any machine, today and in two years.
  • No charting library. The charts are hand-written SVG. That sounds like extra work until you try to make a library produce 2px gaps between stacked segments and rounded ends only at the data end. Nothing on the page loads from a CDN except the two typefaces, and those have declared fallbacks.
  • Four sources, joined and validated. Matches, squads and stadiums all come from the same public-domain feed, plus Natural Earth coastlines for the venue map. Squad lists give every scorer a position and a date of birth, which is where "which kind of player scores, and at what age" comes from. Same licence, no API key, so the join costs nothing — but 178 scorer names had to be matched against squad lists, and a wrong match files a striker's goals under a defender. Exact matching got 148. Folding accents got 162. Two team aliases and six hand-checked transliterations got the rest. Nothing is fuzzy-matched: a name that will not join fails the build rather than landing in an "Unknown" bucket.
  • The colours were validated, not chosen. The palette was run through a colourblind-separation check in both light and dark modes rather than eyeballed. Score state is encoded as diverging — trailing and leading are opposite poles — because it's polarity, not identity.
  • The raw feed is committed. The build reproduces byte-for-byte even if upstream changes.

What this data can't tell you

Two things I wanted are published nowhere in the public-domain sources, at any price:

  • Attendance. The Venue view shows seats instead, labelled as capacity — it answers "how big is this ground", not "how full was it", and the axis says so. Calling capacity attendance would have been the easy lie.
  • Coaches. The Country header carries flag, confederation and group, and stops there rather than pulling a name off a wiki page I can't validate against anything.

Position and age I did get, because the same public-domain repo also publishes the 48 squad lists — no paid lookup involved. That join is the work described above, and it's why the Goal Insights view can plot scorers by age and position at all.
